Our research

The Problem

Short-form video and algorithm-driven feeds are trapping teens in a cycle of overstimulation, isolation, and compulsive use. Here's what we've found, nationally and in our own community.

A digital disease, close to home

In recent years, short-form video content has taken the world by storm, especially among young teenagers. The algorithms that recommend content are built to keep users on the platform, not to help them.

According to Pew Research Center, the share of American teens who say they're on social media "almost constantly" has nearly doubled over the past decade, from 24% to 46%. Over the same period, the CDC reports that roughly 40% of students experience persistent feelings of sadness and hopelessness.

Together, these trends form an overstimulation cycle that traps teenagers in an "endless" loop of addiction and declining mental health.

"It definitely makes me procrastinate any work I have, especially if the work is not due immediately. Social media also really lowers my confidence, but it's pretty addicting at the same time." 9th-grade student, West Windsor-Plainsboro
In WW-P

What our community told us

From November 9 to 11, 2026, 188 students in grades 6 through 12 across the West Windsor-Plainsboro Regional School District answered our survey. Here's what they told us.

86%

open an app "for a few minutes" and end up on it longer than planned, at least sometimes. 30% say it happens almost every day.

52%

are still on their phones at 10 PM or later on school nights. 9% stay on until midnight or later.

66%

named short-form video (TikTok, YouTube Shorts, Reels) as one of the things they spend the most free time on.

43%

feel worse after a long scroll, and about half of them keep scrolling anyway.

49%

use their phones to escape feeling stressed or lonely.

40%

wouldn't talk to anyone if they were going through a hard time. They'd handle it alone or ignore it.

Percentages are out of all 188 responses. Students could pick more than one app type and more than one feeling.

Asking for help

What stops students from reaching out

When we asked who they'd talk to during a hard time, only 5 of 188 students (3%) said a teacher or counselor. The most common answers were a parent (30%) or a friend (27%), and 40% said they'd talk to no one or just ignore it.

72% named at least one thing that would make asking for help hard. The biggest barriers weren't about access. They were about feeling awkward, judged, or like a burden.

That's why Unspooled focuses on the whole cycle, including the loneliness that sends teens back to their phones, not just screen time.

How the trap works

The Overstimulation Cycle

Each stage feeds the next, trapping students in a chokehold of addiction and guilt.

Overstimulation

Content apps deliver quick dopamine spikes through likes, streaks, and infinite feeds.

Real Life Feels Dull

Compared to scrolling, homework, sports, hobbies, and conversation start to feel boring. Time spent with other people drops.

Isolation & Decline

Disconnection from the real world breeds loneliness, which can compound into anxiety and depression.

Escape to the Phone

When reaching out feels hard, teens fall back on social media to cope, and the loop restarts.

↻ Left unchecked, the loop erodes academic performance and self-control.

Less time together

According to the American Time Use Survey, over the last 20 years, 15–24 year-olds went from spending about an hour a day with other people to only 35 minutes. Researchers who have followed teens since the 1970s have found that the less face-to-face time teens have, the lonelier they feel.

Real mental health risk

In 2023, the U.S. Surgeon General warned that adolescents who spend more than 3 hours per day on social media face double the risk of mental health problems, including symptoms of depression and anxiety.

Who's affected

Everyone has a stake

This issue reaches far beyond the individual student.

Teens

The people most directly caught in the cycle, academically, socially, and emotionally.

Parents

Often struggle to model healthy digital habits themselves.

Teachers & Administrators

See the effects in class, while many schools rely on technology that can undermine focus.

School Counselors

Limited counseling resources often restrict students' access to professional support.

Technology Companies

Design the algorithms and engagement features that drive compulsive use.

Our Community

Benefits when young people are healthier, more connected, and more productive.

The gap

Why current solutions fall short

Existing tools aim to limit screen time, but they often don't address the addiction cycle as a whole.

SolutionWhat it doesWhere it falls short
NJ Bell-to-Bell Phone BanRestricts phone use during the school day.Doesn't reach evenings and late nights, when much of the compulsive scrolling happens.
Screen-time apps (e.g., Opal)Block or limit apps on a schedule.Often behind paywalls, and easy for students to bypass or turn off.
Physical devices (e.g., Brick)Lock distracting apps until the phone is scanned on a device.Costs money, and only treats the symptom rather than the reasons teens reach for their phones.
"I think we should be allowed to use our phones at lunch and study hall, because this could prevent the amount of usage after school." 9th grader, on the school phone ban
"They should learn to use it responsibly, be able to manage their time, and be aware enough to realize they are doomscrolling and put their phone down." 9th grader, on what they'd change

Students had mixed feelings about the school phone ban. Several told us it doesn't really change how much students use their phones, and one suggested it might even lead to more scrolling after school. Others said students need to learn to manage their phones themselves.

Unspooled aims to fill that gap: free, community-based approaches that go after every stage of the cycle, from the pull of the feed to the loneliness that sends teens back to it.

"Man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains." Jean-Jacques Rousseau, The Social Contract (1762)

Today, addictive platforms act as those chains on teens' focus, sleep, and well-being. Unspooled exists to help loosen them.
